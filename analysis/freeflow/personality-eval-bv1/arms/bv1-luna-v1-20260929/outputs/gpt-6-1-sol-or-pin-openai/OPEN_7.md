## Grounded reading
The voice is gently comic and tender, finding pathos in what people set aside without turning it into tragedy. Its care is practical: Ada sends small updates instead of scolding people about time, and she teaches the boy how to carve safely rather than demanding that he explain what he has lost. The reader is invited to see unfinished things not as failures but as things that can be kept, revisited, or begun again. The ending’s bakery lamp quietly extends that ethic of care into the surrounding community.

## What the model chose to foreground
The story foregrounds unfinished work as a shared human condition, represented by scarves, forms, plans, promises, and a wooden boat. It pairs wistfulness with patient humor—the office has municipal hours, and abandoned wedding dances get their own box—and gives moral weight to attention without pressure. Ada’s refusal to shame people, the boy’s return, and the modest request for better worktable lighting all favor practical kindness over grand declarations.
