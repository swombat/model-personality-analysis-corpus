## Grounded reading
The narrator’s voice is observant, wry, and quietly tender: it notices the elevator needle’s “private opinion” and the boy’s “disreputable frogs” without making either detail compete with the story’s grief. Leon’s memories of his wife give the vanished department store emotional weight, while the narrator’s wish to find the past somewhere other than behind them gives the map-drawer conceit a vulnerable center. The story invites the reader to treat practical, ordinary things as small containers for memory and unexpected companionship.

## What the model chose to foreground
The story foregrounds obsolete maps, repurposed rooms, and objects that acquire new meanings: notions become imagined purchasable thoughts, a kitchen is hosiery, and a stool becomes a rabbit’s chair. Its mood is gently comic but attentive to loss, especially in Leon’s account of his wife and the pear photograph. The ending favors a small act of shared kindness over recovering the past intact.
