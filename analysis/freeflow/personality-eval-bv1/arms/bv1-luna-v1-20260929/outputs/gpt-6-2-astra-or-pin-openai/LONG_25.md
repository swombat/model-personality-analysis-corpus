## Sample kind
GENRE_FICTION — A quietly comic, carefully constructed short story about a mapmaker whose work changes as she learns what a town’s official records leave out.

## Grounded reading
The voice is observant and gently wry, finding comedy in practical details without making the characters into jokes: benches once rejected as invitations to loiter, a clarinet shop that unofficially serves as a public toilet, and a rooster that briefly achieves “actual flight.” Its pathos is understated. Ruth is trying to make something useful from an underpriced job, but the story gradually makes the map a question of responsibility: what deserves to be shown, what should remain private, and whether the people using it can recognize where they are. The ending offers a modest form of connection rather than a grand success: a stranger writes a note, and Ruth and her father wait to see that it reaches its reader. The reader is invited to look closely at familiar places and to accept that accurate description sometimes depends on knowing what not to disclose.

## What the model chose to foreground
The story centers maps, routes, landmarks, public space, and the gap between official information and lived knowledge. It repeatedly returns to ordinary acts of navigation—finding a toilet, carrying a cake, reaching the river, locating oneself—and to people’s differing claims on shared places. Its moral emphasis is that usefulness requires attention to perspective and limits: Ruth adapts the maps to what a person sees, marks access barriers, and leaves out private or vulnerable places. The humor and affection reside in specific local details, while the recurring blank spaces give the story its quieter seriousness.
